Chapter 1. 1923, Kanpur, India

Born in Kanpur

Harish-Chandra was born in Kanpur on 11 October 1923. His father, Chandrakishore, was a civil engineer in the irrigation service of what was then the United Provinces; his mother, Chandrarani, was the daughter of a lawyer.

His father spent much of his time travelling to inspect canals, so the boy grew up largely in his maternal grandfather’s home in Kanpur. He was often unwell, a great deal of care went into his education at home, and he did not enter school until he was nine.

Chapter 2. 1932–39, Kanpur

An early start

Once he started school he moved quickly. He completed Christ Church High School at fourteen and intermediate college in Kanpur at sixteen.

His biographers describe a high-strung boy, often ill, who was conspicuously quick at his studies. By sixteen he was ready for university.

Chapter 3. 1939–43, University of Allahabad

Physics at Allahabad

At the University of Allahabad he took a B.Sc. in 1941 and an M.Sc. in 1943, at the age of twenty. In 1940, in the university library, he came across Paul Dirac’s book on quantum mechanics. It made him want to become a theoretical physicist.

The physicist K. S. Krishnan, who became professor at Allahabad in 1942, encouraged him in every way and lent him Hermann Weyl’s Raum-Zeit-Materie. For the M.Sc. he was examined by C. V. Raman, and he scored full marks on the written examination.

04

Chapter 4. 1943–45, Bangalore

Research with Bhabha

Krishnan recommended him to Homi J. Bhabha, and Harish-Chandra moved to the Indian Institute of Science in Bangalore. For his first six months there he lived with Dr Kale, a botanist from Allahabad, and his wife, who had taught him French.

With Bhabha he worked on relativistic wave equations and the physics of point particles, and some of their papers were written jointly. C. V. Raman, also in Bangalore, would call on him unannounced to invite him for a walk.

How Harishchandra became Harish-Chandra

Historical fact
Harish-cChandra

Harishchandra, as he was named

Bhabha sent the papers from Bangalore to the Proceedings of the Royal Society. A copy editor there split the name with a hyphen, and he decided to keep it.

A legendary name. Harishchandra is also the name of a king in Indian legend, remembered for his devotion to truth.

Chapter 5. 1945, At sea, India to England

Passage to England

Bhabha and Krishnan recommended him to Paul Dirac, who accepted him as a research student at Cambridge. Once the war in Europe was over, he sailed for England.

Dirac already knew his work. Because wartime mail between India and England was slow, Bhabha had asked Dirac to correct the proofs of their papers for the Royal Society.

Chapter 6. 1945–47, Cambridge, England

A doctorate under Dirac

At Gonville and Caius College, Cambridge, he worked for his doctorate under Dirac, who suggested the problem: find the infinite-dimensional irreducible representations of the Lorentz group, the symmetry group of special relativity. His thesis of 1947 was titled Infinite irreducible representations of the Lorentz group. He later said this was how he got started in group representations.

In 1946 he pointed out a mistake in a lecture by Wolfgang Pauli; the two later became friends. When he complained that his own proofs were not rigorous, Dirac answered that he was not interested in proofs, only in what nature does.

Representations you can turn in your hand

Exact computation, simplified setting

Drag the sphere, or focus it and use the arrow keys.

His thesis problem asked for the irreducible representations of the Lorentz group. That group contains the ordinary rotations of space, and for rotations the idea can be seen directly on a sphere.

Each colour pattern on the sphere belongs to a family of 2ℓ+1 patterns. Turn the sphere and the pattern changes, but it can always be rebuilt from the patterns of its own family, and from no others. A closed family like this is an irreducible representation of the rotation group.

    1.000Sum of squared coordinates inside the family
    0.000Amount leaking into other families

    Why it mattered. For rotations every family is finite. For the Lorentz group, apart from the trivial one, the irreducible unitary representations are infinite-dimensional, which is what made his thesis problem hard.

    Chapter 7. 1947–50, Princeton and Harvard

    From physics to mathematics

    In 1947 Dirac took him to the Institute for Advanced Study in Princeton as his assistant. Harish-Chandra stayed a second year after Dirac returned to Cambridge, and then spent 1949 at Harvard.

    In Princeton he followed Claude Chevalley’s courses and turned decisively to mathematics. He also spotted a gap in a proof in André Weil’s book on integration in topological groups.

    Symmetry where order matters

    Simplified picture
    Turn, then flip
    Flip, then turn

    Both triangles start the same, gold corner on top. The turn is 120° clockwise; the flip is across the dashed line.

    Fourier’s waves live on a line, where moves can be made in any order. The symmetries of a triangle are different: turn it and then flip it, and you get a different result from flipping first.

    Groups in which the order of moves matters are called non-commutative. The large continuous ones that matter most in geometry and physics, the semisimple Lie groups, became his territory.

    Chapter 8. 1950–63, New York City

    The Columbia years

    In 1950 he joined Columbia University, his base until 1963. In a long paper of 1951 he proved what is now called the Harish-Chandra isomorphism: the centre of the universal enveloping algebra of a complex semisimple Lie algebra corresponds exactly to the polynomials on a Cartan subalgebra that are unchanged by the Weyl group.

    By 1954 he had shown that every irreducible representation of a semisimple Lie group appears inside a member of the principal series, and he had begun to treat characters as distributions. The American Mathematical Society awarded him the Cole Prize in Algebra in 1954.

    The Weyl group, and what it leaves unchanged

    Simplified picture

    Left. Gold arrows are long roots, cyan arrows short ones. One root is ringed so you can follow it. Tap any arrow to reflect the whole picture in the mirror at right angles to it.

    Right. The shading shows the polynomial x³ − 3xy², which takes the same value at all six white dots, the images of one moving point under the six symmetries. Polynomials with this property are exactly the ones in his 1951 isomorphism.

    Reading it. Each semisimple Lie group has a finite set of arrows called roots. Reflections in the mirrors at right angles to them generate the Weyl group, and the whole pattern always lands on itself.

    Chapter 9. 1952–53, Bangalore and Bombay

    Marriage in Bangalore

    On a visit to India in 1952 he married Lalitha (Lily) Kale of Bangalore. C. V. Raman met him at the airport when he arrived. The couple later had two daughters.

    He spent 1952–53 at the Tata Institute of Fundamental Research in Bombay, the institute Bhabha had founded, before returning to Columbia.

    Chapter 10. 1957–58, Paris, France

    Paris and André Weil

    A Guggenheim Fellowship took him to Paris for 1957–58. He and André Weil took long walks together, and Weil’s lectures set him on the problem of arithmetic groups.

    That work led to a joint paper with Armand Borel, Arithmetic subgroups of algebraic groups (Annals of Mathematics, 1962), and it fed into the idea now known as his philosophy of cusp forms, borrowed from the theory of automorphic forms.

    People in this chapter

    • AWAndré WeilWalks in Paris

    Cusp forms fade at the cusp

    Simplified picture

    Figure. Shaded: a fundamental domain of the modular group SL(2,ℤ) in the upper half-plane. As the probe climbs towards the cusp, the size of a cusp form (gold) dies away rapidly while an Eisenstein series (cyan) keeps its constant term.

    Historical fact

    He borrowed the idea of cusp forms from the theory of automorphic forms and recast harmonic analysis on real groups in its terms. Later he carried the same philosophy to p-adic groups and, in 1970, to finite groups of Lie type.

    The picture. On the modular surface a cusp form is a symmetric function that fades away as you climb into the narrow cusp at the top. Cusp forms are the basic pieces from which the rest is assembled.

    Chapter 11. 1963–66, Princeton, New Jersey

    The discrete series

    In 1963 he moved to the Institute for Advanced Study, where he lectured most years on research still in progress. His first lectures on the discrete series, in 1961, had been broken off when he found a gap; he resumed them two years later, once it was closed.

    In Discrete series for semisimple Lie groups I and II (1965–66) he proved that a group has a discrete series exactly when it has a compact Cartan subgroup, and he determined the characters of these representations. His regularity theorem, announced in 1963, showed that characters are given by locally integrable functions.

    Two ideas from these years

    Model computations

    Figure. Model computation for SL(2,ℝ). Coefficients of the n-th discrete series decay like e−nt while the group’s volume grows like sinh 2t. The running total settles for n = 2 and n = 3, but keeps growing for the continuous family.

    Discrete series. A representation belongs to the discrete series when its matrix coefficients die away fast enough to be square-integrable over the whole group. Such pieces sit inside the functions on the group as isolated notes.

    What he proved. A group has a discrete series exactly when it has a compact Cartan subgroup.

    Chapter 12. 1968–81, Princeton and abroad

    Recognition

    In 1968 he was named IBM–von Neumann Professor at the Institute. He was elected a Fellow of the Royal Society in 1973, received the Srinivasa Ramanujan Medal of the Indian National Science Academy in 1974, and became a member of the US National Academy of Sciences.

    The honours came on top of the Cole Prize of 1954. Through all of it, his days kept the same shape: mathematics, and a long walk.

    Chapter 13. 1975–76, Princeton

    The Plancherel formula

    For about a quarter of a century he worked towards one goal: an explicit Plancherel formula, the exact recipe for breaking a function on a semisimple group into irreducible representations. He had already proved it for the group SL(2,ℝ) in 1952.

    He completed it for all real reductive groups in three long papers, Harmonic analysis on real reductive groups I–III, published in 1975–76. He also carried his methods over to p-adic groups, the analogues that matter in number theory.

    From Fourier series to the Plancherel formula

    Simplified picture, then the real measure
    3 waves
    Energy captured: 93.3%

    Step 1. Dashed: a square wave. Gold: the sum of the pure waves so far. Cyan: the newest wave. The meter shows Parseval’s identity at work: the energies of the pieces add up exactly to the energy of the whole.

    Step 2. The Plancherel measure of SL(2,ℝ), as he computed it in 1952, shown up to normalising constants. Top: discrete series n = 2, 3, 4 … with weight n − 1. Bottom: the two continuous families, with densities λ·tanh(πλ/2) and λ·coth(πλ/2).

    The connection. For Fourier series every frequency gets equal weight. On a semisimple group the spectrum has isolated notes and continuous bands, each with its own weight. The Plancherel formula is the exact accounting, and his general version is the far-reaching generalisation of what the meter shows.

    Interlude: why it matters today

    Why it matters today

    A note for students of AI, data science and mathematics. He did not work on computing; the links below are shared mathematical language, not direct descent.

    Fourier analysis everywhere

    Audio compression, image processing and MRI reconstruction all split signals into frequencies. Harmonic analysis on groups, his field, generalises that idea to spaces with symmetry.

    Symmetry in machine learning

    Group-equivariant neural networks are designed so their outputs change predictably when an image or molecule is rotated or shifted. Their design uses group representations, the theory he helped build for infinite-dimensional cases.

    These networks were developed decades after his death, by other researchers.

    Symmetry and prime numbers

    The Langlands programme, one of the largest projects in modern mathematics, draws on his harmonic analysis and his philosophy of cusp forms to connect symmetry with number theory.

    Chapter 14. 1983, Princeton, New Jersey

    A last walk

    From 1969 he suffered a series of heart attacks, yet he kept up his long afternoon walks. In October 1983 a conference in honour of Armand Borel was held in Princeton, and, as Langlands recalled, for that week his old vigour returned.

    He died on 16 October 1983, during a walk, five days after his sixtieth birthday.

    In mathematics we agree that clear thinking is very important, but fuzzy thinking is just as important as clear thinking.

    Harish-Chandra, at a large gathering the day before his death

    In memory of Harish-Chandra

    Kanpur, 11 October 1923  to  Princeton, 16 October 1983

    Behind the portrait, the twelve roots of A₃ turn in space, circled by the fourteen years of this story.

    He came to mathematics by way of physics, and stayed with one great question for the rest of his life: how the continuous symmetries of geometry and physics break into their simplest pieces. His answers, built slowly and with great care, are foundations that others still build on.

    • The Plancherel formulaCompleted for every real reductive group in three papers of 1975–76.
    • The discrete seriesFound, with their characters, in 1965–66.
    • Collected PapersFour volumes, edited by V. S. Varadarajan, Springer, 1984.
    • A name that continuesThe Harish-Chandra Research Institute in Prayagraj carries his name.

    Langlands counted him among the people who turned infinite-dimensional representation theory from a modest side topic into a field at the centre of modern mathematics.
